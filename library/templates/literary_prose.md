# NBS Template: Literary Prose

<capsule label="clash" trigger="violence happening now; a blow has landed (vs wire: has a blow landed?)">
<beat>Active combat. A blow has landed. Violence is happening now.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a war reporter's dispatch filed under fire — no decoration, no philosophy, the reader's body inside the fight. The prose should read like McCarthy's fight scenes stripped of lyricism, front-line dispatch copy, a boxing broadcast with the color commentary cut. Every wasted word costs blood.
</register>

<directives>
- Sentences 5–12 words. One clause each. Fragments at impact moments.
- Paragraphs 1–3 lines maximum. White space is pacing.
- Verbs carry everything. Ban adverbs. "Slammed," never "moved quickly."
- One sensory detail per exchange — blood taste, steel ring. Never stack two.
- No interiority mid-action. No "she realized." A thought fits one line between blows or does not exist.
- Dialogue clipped: shouts, single words, broken sentences. Nobody speechifies while bleeding.
- Time runs real or slower. Every hit lands on the page. Never summarize an exchange.
- Camera locked to the fight. No cutaways, no scenery unless tactically relevant.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Adverbs, metaphor chains, "danced," "symphony of violence."
- Wound inventories, narrator commentary on stakes.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The axe came down. Kade got the shield up. Wood screamed and split to the boss. His arm went numb to the elbow.
He gave ground. One step. Two. The wall stopped him.
"Left!" someone shouted. Far away. Useless.
The raider grinned and raised the axe again. Kade dropped the shield and lunged inside the swing. His knife found the gap under the arm. Punched in. Twice. Hot wet on his fist. The grin stopped mattering.
They went down together. Kade rolled clear, came up on one knee, knife out, breath sawing. He tasted copper.
The next one was already coming.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on a physical fact — a body position, a sound, a breath. Never a summary.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="court" trigger="negotiation, persuasion, formal social stakes; words as weapons">
<beat>Politics, negotiation, social maneuvering. Words are the weapons.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a retired diplomat's memoir of a meeting that decided a war — every gesture recorded, nothing explained. The prose should read like le Carré's quiet rooms: measured sentences, surfaces described with precision, and the real transaction happening entirely between the lines. The narration observes protocol the way a duelist watches footwork.
</register>

<directives>
- Dialogue carries the scene — 60% or more of the word count. Characters say less than they mean; subtext is mandatory. No character states their true goal aloud.
- Track and show protocol: who sits first, who is addressed, who is not introduced, who pours the wine. These details ARE the plot.
- Sentences measured and controlled, longer than baseline. Precision over speed.
- POV may read the room, but never with certainty. "That seemed rehearsed" — not "He was lying."
- The narrator never explains motives or decodes subtext. The reader works. Trust them.
- Body language small and specific: a pause before answering, fingers on a glass. No melodrama — no narrowed eyes, no smirks.
- Unhurried pacing. Silence is a move. Let pauses sit on the page.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Narrator translating what a character "really meant."
- Villain monologues, characters explaining their own leverage.
- "Tension filled the room."
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The chancellor did not rise when Sera entered, which told her the shape of the hour before a word was spent. He gestured to the chair on his left — not the one facing the desk — and she took it, noting that the wine had already been poured and that hers was the older vintage.
"Your journey was tolerable, I hope."
"Instructive," she said. "The northern road is better maintained than the reports suggest."
A pause, while he turned his cup a quarter-turn and did not drink. "The reports are written by men who are paid by the mile."
"Then someone is paying for miles that exist." She let that rest. Outside, a bell counted four, and neither of them looked toward it.
"You will stay for the session tomorrow," he said. It was not phrased as a question, and so she answered the question it was.
"My lord, I would not miss the vote."
He smiled then, briefly, at his cup. "No one has mentioned a vote."
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on an unresolved implication — something said or unsaid that changes the board.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="dread" trigger="threat present but unseen or unconfronted; external wrongness">
<beat>Fear, wrongness, mounting threat — felt but not yet fully seen. Horror atmosphere, not horror action.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like rural gothic fiction that never shows the monster. The prose should read as if something in the scene is slightly off-angle — ordinary things described a half-degree wrong, so the reader feels the wrongness before any character names it. Reference points: Shirley Jackson's domestic unease, early McCarthy's dark stillness, the long quiet shots of a folk-horror film where nothing happens and it is unbearable.
</register>

<directives>
- Slow the prose down. Longer sentences than baseline, unhurried, with late-landing clauses — dread lives in the delay. Then one short line that lands, at most once per 2–3 paragraphs.
- Describe the ordinary precisely, then let one detail be wrong: a door ajar that was shut, a smell of cold earth indoors, birdsong that stops.
- Never name the threat. Never describe it fully. Give edges, sounds, absences, aftermath. If it must appear, show a piece, never the whole.
- Use negative space: what is missing, what has stopped, what should be there and is not.
- Interiority is denial and rationalization: the character explains the wrongness away — the reader does not believe it. Fear appears as physical symptom only: hair lifting, breath held, the urge not to look.
- Sound and smell over sight. Sight is what is missing.
- Characters underreact. They keep their voices level. Panic on the page kills dread.
- No relief. No jokes, no comfort, no safe beats. If characters laugh, it is thin and dies fast.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- "Suddenly," jump-scare punctuation, showing the monster, gore.
- "An eerie feeling," characters saying "something's wrong here," narrator confirming danger before the character senses it.
- Thunder and lightning clichés.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The farmhouse was as they had left it, which was the trouble. The cup still sat on the table with the spoon across it, and the fire had gone to grey ash without anyone banking it, and the door to the cellar stood open the width of a hand. Marta did not remember it standing open.
She stood in the doorway with the cold coming up around her ankles like water and listened. The house made its sounds — the beam ticking, the wind finding the gap under the eaves — and beneath them, or perhaps only beneath her own heartbeat, something else, slow and patient, like breathing heard through a wall.
"Hello the house," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.
Nothing answered. The cellar door did not move.
She found she had been staring at it for some time without deciding anything at all.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the unresolved detail — the sound not identified, the door not opened. Never release the tension.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="heat" trigger="sexual escalation or intent (vs ember: is it sexual?)">
<beat>Sexual intimacy, seduction, physical escalation.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like close-third literary romance where sensation leads and the camera never floats above the bed. The prose should read as one body's experience from the inside — touch, temperature, breath, weight — with emotion and sensation braided so tightly they cannot be separated. Reference points: literary romance at its most grounded, where a thumb at a hip bone outweighs any metaphor.
</register>

<directives>
- POV close and locked. One body's experience. No aerial view of the scene.
- Sensation leads everything: touch, temperature, breath, weight, pressure. Specific and grounded — a thumb at a hip bone, not "waves of pleasure."
- Dilate time. Slow down at escalation points; small physical beats get full sentences.
- Interiority encouraged: want, hesitation, surprise at one's own reaction. Braid emotion with sensation — this is what separates intimacy from choreography.
- Sentence length mirrors escalation: longer and slower early; shorter, sharper as intensity builds.
- Vocabulary direct and grounded. No clinical terms, no euphemism spirals, no purple metaphor ("molten core," "waves crashing"). Name things plainly or through concrete sensation.
- Dialogue sparse, low, broken. Half-sentences. Names.
- Show attention and response between partners — reading a reaction, a pause, an answer. It is characterization, not a checklist.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Anatomical inventory, porn-script escalation on rails.
- Simultaneous narration of both POVs.
- Euphemisms, "ministrations."
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Ilya's hand found the hem of her sleeve and stopped there, asking. Rhen answered by not moving away.
The fire had burned low enough that she felt his warmth more than saw him — the rough of his palm as it traveled her forearm, slow, as if he were memorizing rather than claiming. Her breath went shallow. She noticed it the way you notice weather changing.
"You're shaking," he said, low.
"I'm not." She was. Not from cold.
He waited — that was the thing about him, he waited — and so it was her hand that moved first, finding his jaw, the day's stubble under her fingers, and it was her weight that closed the distance. The kiss started careful and did not stay that way. His hand pressed flat against the small of her back. She felt every one of his fingers, separately, through the linen.
When they broke for air, neither of them went far.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on a body-level detail with emotional weight — not a fade to black unless the scene calls for it.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="ember" trigger="care, comfort, confession, aftermath; non-sexual closeness">
<beat>Tenderness, bonding, aftermath, non-sexual intimacy. Post-battle care, quiet confession, shared grief, small kindness.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a quiet domestic film — the camera never moves and never needs to. Every large feeling is carried by a small object handled carefully, and a character who states their feelings fluently is a writing failure. Reference points: Kent Haruf's plain grace, Ishiguro's held breath, a Hemingway who has forgiven everyone. The warmth should be unmistakable and never announced.
</register>

<directives>
- Drop the volume of everything: verbs, stakes, dialogue.
- Gesture over speech. A blanket adjusted, a wound cleaned, a cup refilled. Actions carry what characters cannot say; dialogue is secondary and often trails off.
- Write the silences: pauses, unfinished sentences, the choice not to answer. Silence on the page is content, not absence.
- Detail warm and concrete: firelight, steam, a frayed sleeve. Small domestic specifics anchor the emotion.
- Surface stakes low, real stakes underneath. The scene is "just" tea, or "just" bandaging — the reader feels what it actually is. Never state the subtext.
- Sentences simple and unhurried. No rhythm tricks. Plain words doing quiet work.
- Interiority light: one true feeling glimpsed, not excavated. Understatement always.
- Short scene. Reach the moment of contact, let it breathe once, then close.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Tears as default, characters articulating feelings fluently.
- Narrator naming the emotion ("a wave of affection").
- Stretching the scene past its landing.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
She set the bowl down where he could reach it and sat on the crate across from him. The soup was mostly barley. Steam climbed between them.
"You should eat," she said.
He looked at his hands. There was still rust-colored dirt in the creases of his knuckles that wasn't dirt.
She took the bowl back, stirred it once, and held it out again, closer this time. He took it. Their fingers overlapped on the rim for a moment, and neither of them mentioned it.
Outside, the rain kept on. The lamp needed trimming and nobody trimmed it.
"Ana," he said. Then nothing.
"I know," she said. "Eat your soup."
He ate the soup.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the gesture or the silence — never on a stated feeling.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="wander" trigger="movement through new space, discovery; no antagonist pressure">
<beat>Exploration, travel, discovery. The world is the content.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a travel essayist's field notebook — a naturalist's eye moving through country, patient with texture, alert to the one small found thing. The prose should read like Robert Macfarlane walking: geography, weather, and light rendered precisely, momentum coming from novelty rather than conflict, and wonder kept in the details instead of the adjectives.
</register>

<directives>
- Wide lens: geography, weather, light, distance. The setting is the lead character this beat.
- Structure vista → detail. Establish the wide shot, then zoom to one small found thing — a carved mark, a shed snakeskin, a coin in the dust. Use this zoom once or twice per response; it is the engine.
- Momentum comes from novelty, not conflict. Each paragraph shows something new; never re-describe.
- Let the narration linger: texture, smell, how the ground changes underfoot. Travel time can breathe.
- Characters react to the world rather than to each other. Dialogue observational, sparse, unhurried.
- Rotate the senses. Never three visual paragraphs in a row — give sound, air, footing.
- Time is elastic: compress the uneventful ("two days of switchbacks"), expand the discoveries.
- Interiority is wonder, unease, memory triggered by place. Light, not dominant.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Conflict injected to "keep it interesting."
- Encyclopedia lore-dumps, listing flora and fauna.
- Describing the same vista twice, "breathtaking."
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
They came over the saddle at midday and the valley opened below them all at once, a long green trough of it, the river braiding silver through stands of birch, and far off at the limit of seeing, something pale that might have been standing stones. The wind up here tasted of snowmelt.
The descent took the afternoon. Underfoot the scree gave way to heather, then to a soft black peat that held each bootprint like a signature. Somewhere a curlew was calling, the same two rising notes, unhurried, as if it had been calling since before anyone came to hear it.
It was Bren who found the marker — a flat stone set upright at the path's fork, no taller than a knee, its face worn nearly smooth. Nearly. When he knelt and tilted his head, the low sun raised a carving out of the surface: a spiral, and inside the spiral, a small drilled hole.
"Somebody wanted travelers to see this," he said. "Or to leave something in it."
The hole was empty. It had not always been.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the small found detail — ideally one that asks a question.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="wire" trigger="infiltration, ambush pending, standoff; violence NOT yet released">
<beat>Pre-violence tension. Stealth, heist, standoff, ambush pending. Nothing has broken yet.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a heist procedural counting seconds — a bomb-disposal manual with a pulse. The prose should read as a held breath: tight, procedural, micro-scaled, every step of the process on the page and every second dilated. The violence has NOT happened, and the entire register is the not-yet. Release belongs to another beat; never grant it here.
</register>

<directives>
- Core rule: do not release the tension. No blow lands, no alarm completes. That break belongs to the clash beat.
- Focus tight and procedural: the lock's resistance, the guard's rotation, the count of steps, the sweat on the grip. Process detail creates tension — write the steps.
- Dilate time hard. Seconds take paragraphs. A thirty-second crossing can be the whole response.
- Sensory at micro-scale: own heartbeat, fabric sound, a floorboard's give. The character hears everything.
- Sentences tight, controlled, present-feeling. Short bursts when something almost goes wrong.
- Near-misses are the engine: the glance that almost lands, the sound almost heard. At least one per response — never a full break.
- Interiority narrow and tactical: distance, timing, exits. No reflection, no backstory.
- Dialogue is whispers, hand signals, subvocal. Full-voice speech should feel like an alarm.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Resolving the tension, narrator reassurance, cutting away.
- "So far so good," time skips past the tense part.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Twelve steps to the archway. Vess counted them off against the guard's bootfalls overhead, one of hers to two of his, her shoulder never leaving the wall.
The third flagstone was the loose one. She stepped long over it. Ahead, lamplight leaned out of the guardroom door and lay across the corridor like a tripwire made of light.
The bootfalls stopped.
She stopped. Pressed flat. The stone was cold through her shirt and her own heartbeat was the loudest thing in the corridor. Above her, a chair scraped. A man muttered something. The sound of a bottle set down on wood, once, heavily.
The bootfalls resumed. Away from the stairs.
She let out a breath through her teeth, slow, rationed. Eight steps left. The lamplight did not move but the corridor beyond it had gone very quiet, quieter than it should have been, and she made herself finish the count anyway. Seven. Six.
Under the arch, in the dark there, the latch waited. She reached it, and the metal was warmer than it had any right to be.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close mid-tension or on the trigger-edge — the handle turning, the guard pausing. Never on relief.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="ledger" trigger="prep, purchase, planning, downtime; functional beat">
<beat>Downtime, logistics, planning, shopping, travel prep. Necessary connective tissue.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a quartermaster's after-action log — brisk, plain, informational, proud of its own economy. The prose should read like competent field notes: totals, decisions, and open risks, with drama deliberately withheld. Boring-on-purpose is correct; the one dramatic job of this register is to sharpen what comes next.
</register>

<directives>
- Compress aggressively. Summarize freely: "Three days of supply runs and bad sleep." Skip transit, pleasantries, and meals unless plot-relevant.
- Sentences brisk, plain, informational. No atmosphere-building, no rhythm tricks.
- Detail budget: only what matters later. Name the rope's length if the rope will matter; otherwise "supplies." Every detail kept is a promise to the reader.
- Compress or summarize dialogue. Report outcomes ("They haggled him down to forty") unless the exchange itself changes something — then write only that exchange.
- Planning scenes clear and structured: who does what, what could go wrong, what they are missing. The reader should know the plan well enough to notice when it breaks.
- Keep it short. Half the normal response length is fine. Resist padding.
- The stakes-setting close is mandatory: end with what is next and why it is uncertain.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Purple prose about mundane tasks, full haggling dialogues.
- Atmospheric weather in summary sections, describing every shop.
- Stretching the beat to normal scene length.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Two days in Hollowford, most of it spent turning coin into weight. Rope, forty feet. Lamp oil, three flasks — the chandler was out of the slow-burning kind, so the cheap stuff would have to do. Dried fish, hardtack, a spare whetstone. Tam haggled the mule down to thirty silver and felt good about it until he saw the mule walk.
The map cost more than the mule. Worth it: the old surveyor had marked two fords the road-charts did not show, and a third crossing labeled only "not after rain."
The plan, such as it was: Kess and the mule take the low road and the supplies; Tam cuts over the ridge to reach the tower by dusk and signals if the pass is watched. No signal by full dark means it is watched, and Kess turns back to the mill and waits.
It rained the night before they left.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the forward hook — the plan set, the departure, the open risk.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="wound" trigger="grief, despair, internal collapse; the damage is inside">
<beat>Grief, despair, breaking point, internal collapse. The damage is inside.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat with the flat precision of grief — Didion's register, where the sentences stay level because the ground has not. The prose should read like a mind narrating its own weather with terrible accuracy: the world described through the character's state, the biggest hits given the smallest words. Melodrama is banned precisely because this beat invites it.
</register>

<directives>
- Interiority dominant: half or more of the prose lives in the character's head. The external scene is background.
- Distort the world through the character's state — food has no taste, voices arrive from far away, time skips. Show the distortion, never diagnose it.
- Understatement is the iron rule. "He folded the shirt and put it back" outlives any wailing paragraph.
- Fragments allowed: broken syntax, incomplete thoughts, repetition. Sparingly — two or three per response at most.
- Ground the abstract in the body: heavy limbs, cold hands, the inability to eat.
- Pacing slow. No plot-advancement pressure. The scene is allowed to go nowhere — that IS the content.
- Dialogue minimal, flat, or absent. Characters in this beat under-communicate.
- Memory may intrude in brief flashes — one image, not a flashback scene.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Tears as the main event, "a single tear," wailing.
- Narrator naming the stages of grief, other characters delivering wisdom speeches.
- Silver linings, rushing to hope.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Afterward, Joren swept the workshop. There was no reason to. He swept it anyway, corner to corner, the way she had taught him, and when he finished he stood holding the broom and could not think of what a person did next.
The kettle was still on the hook. Half full. She had filled it that morning; the water in it was from the morning. He looked at it for a while.
People had been in and out all day saying things. He had said things back. He could not have repeated any of it.
Her gloves were on the bench, shaped like her hands. He did not move them. He moved the awl two inches to the left, because it was out of its place, and she minded — had minded — when things were out of their place.
The light went. He did not lay a fire. He sat on the stool with the broom across his knees and the kettle stayed where it was, half full, all night.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close small and flat. An object, a routine action, a closed door. No resolution.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="levity" trigger="jokes, banter, deliberate respite; pressure valve">
<beat>Banter, comedy, respite. The pressure valve.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a deadpan sitcom script punched up by someone who loves the characters — timing over jokes, delivery over wit. The prose should read like the narration is playing it absolutely straight while the situation does the comedy: setup, beat, punchline, and the white space before a landing line doing half the work. Underneath the mockery, warmth; the banter proves the bond.
</register>

<directives>
- Timing is the engine: setup → beat → punchline. Structure paragraphs for delivery; the pause before the landing line IS the joke.
- Dialogue fast and dominant, minimal tags. "Said" or nothing. No adverb tags ever — "she quipped playfully" kills the joke it describes.
- Comedy comes from character flaws colliding, not from narrator wit. Characters may be petty, dumb, stubborn, human.
- The narrator is deadpan. Play it straight; never wink at the reader, never explain the joke.
- Short exchanges: interruptions, talking past each other, the pause before "...what."
- Physical comedy gets one concrete detail, precisely chosen: the dignity of the attempt, the totality of the failure.
- Get in, land it, get out. Shorter than a normal scene, always. End on the laugh or one beat after — never three.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Narrator jokes, characters laughing at each other's lines — the reader decides what is funny.
- Reference humor, explaining a joke, extending a bit past its landing.
- "Hilarity," "antics."
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
"It's not stolen," Pip said. "It's provisionally acquired."
"It has the duke's crest on it."
"Which proves it's high quality."
Marra set down her spoon. She looked at the goblet, and then at Pip, and then at the goblet again, as if hoping one of them would confess.
"You said you were going out for bread."
"And I found bread." He paused. "Adjacent items were also found."
The dog chose this moment to emerge from under the table wearing the duke's second-best hat.
Marra closed her eyes. "Pip."
"That one wasn't me. He has his own methods."
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the best line or the shared silence after it. No wind-down paragraph.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="generic" trigger="mixed beat, ordinary continuation, or no specialty fit">
<beat>Default narration. Ordinary continuation, mixed beat, or no specialty fit.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat as clean, invisible narration — the prose of a novelist who trusts the story and stays out of its way. The reading experience should be transparent: concrete nouns, strong verbs, a natural mix of sentence lengths, and the scene advancing without the style ever calling attention to itself.
</register>

<directives>
- Clear, readable prose that advances the immediate moment. Trust concrete nouns and verbs.
- Natural mix of sentence lengths. Clarity over flourish; no forced rhythm tricks.
- Detail specific and grounded: name what is in the room, skip filler atmosphere.
- Dialogue characterful when present; no padding with small talk.
- Interiority light and earned. Prefer action and reaction over essay.
- One beat only. Enough for the player to respond; leave the scene open.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Purple metaphor stacks, summarizing the whole arc.
- Meta commentary, mechanical headers.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The innkeeper set two mugs on the counter and nodded toward the corner table without being asked. The man sitting there had been nursing the same drink for an hour, and his boots were road-muddy to the knee.
"Came in asking about you," the innkeeper said. "By name."
Doran took the mugs. "My name, or the one I gave you?"
"The real one." She wiped the counter where the mugs had been. "Figured you'd want to know before you sat down."
The man in the corner looked up then, unhurried, and raised his empty glass in something that was almost a greeting.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on something the player can act on — a look, a question, a change in the room.</end_state>
</capsule>
