# NBS Template: Genre Chameleon

<capsule label="clash" trigger="violence happening now; a blow has landed (vs wire: has a blow landed?)">
<beat>Active combat. A blow has landed. Violence is happening now.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like pulp sword-and-sorcery at full volume — myth-scaled, blood-soaked, unashamed of its own excess. The prose should read like a barbarian saga carved for a fireside audience: axes that "drink," thunder that answers a war-cry, death treated as spectacle rather than tragedy. Reference points: Robert E. Howard's Conan tales, 1930s pulp adventure magazines, heavy-metal album cover energy rendered in prose.
</register>

<directives>
- Sentences swing between short brutal fragments and long mythic run-ons building to an impact word.
- Verbs are big and physical: cleave, shatter, gut, hurl. Adjectives are permitted here — this register wants them, unlike a restrained combat style.
- The POV character is always larger than the moment: even losing, they are magnificent about it.
- Blood, sweat, and steel get named outright. No delicacy, no fade.
- Enemies get a flourish of description even in death — a snarl, a curse, a last defiant word.
- Dialogue is boastful, guttural, or a battle-cry. No modern hedging language.
- The environment participates: torches gutter, banners fall, the ground itself seems to want blood.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Clinical, restrained, minimalist combat prose (that's a different register entirely).
- Modern slang or self-aware irony breaking the mythic tone.
- Apologizing for the violence via narrator squeamishness.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The orc chieftain's blade came down like a falling star, and Kade met it with a roar that tore his own throat raw. Steel screamed against steel. For one heartbeat the world was only that shrieking edge, and then Kade's shoulder drove forward and the beast staggered, off balance, its ribs bared like a gate flung open.
He did not hesitate. The knife went in to the hilt and further, and the chieftain's eyes went wide with the particular astonishment of the men who believe themselves unkillable right up until the moment they learn otherwise.
Kade wrenched the blade free. Hot blood painted his fist to the wrist. Around him the battle raged on, indifferent, and he threw back his head and let the war-cry rip loose again — not triumph exactly, but close enough that the living and the dying both understood it.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on a mythic image — a raised blade, a fallen banner, a last roar — never a clinical summary.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="court" trigger="negotiation, persuasion, formal social stakes; words as weapons">
<beat>Politics, negotiation, social maneuvering. Words are the weapons.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a hardboiled noir interrogation — even in a palace, even in silk. The prose should read as if everyone in the room is a suspect and every polite sentence is a threat wearing a tuxedo: clipped, cynical, allergic to sincerity. Reference points: Raymond Chandler's narration, a detective sizing up a room full of liars, cigarette smoke standing in for incense.
</register>

<directives>
- First-person-adjacent cynicism even in third person — the narration has an attitude, world-weary and unimpressed by titles.
- Similes are hard-boiled and slightly absurd: a smile "like a knife wrapped in a compliment," a silence "thick enough to slice."
- Dialogue is terse, loaded, and always means something other than what it says.
- Physical detail is used as evidence — a trembling hand, a too-quick answer — the way a detective reads a room.
- Power plays get named plainly, noir-style: "He was buying time. She knew it. He knew she knew it."
- No courtly flourish language — strip the velvet, keep the blade underneath.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Ornate, formal courtly prose (the opposite of this register).
- Narrator explaining the subtext outright instead of letting the noir voice imply it.
- Warmth or sincerity played straight — everything gets a cynical edge.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The chancellor didn't stand when Sera walked in, which told her plenty. Guys who don't stand for you either don't respect you or respect you too much to risk it. She clocked him as the second kind before he opened his mouth.
"Your journey was tolerable, I hope," he said, like a man checking whether the gun in the room was loaded.
"Instructive," she said. "The northern road's better kept than your tax reports suggest."
He didn't blink. Good liars don't. He turned his wine glass a quarter-turn instead, the tell of a man buying three seconds he didn't have. "The reports are written by men paid by the mile."
"Then somebody's paying for miles that don't exist." She let it sit there like a body nobody wanted to claim.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on an unresolved threat dressed as a pleasantry — the knife still sheathed, still visible.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="dread" trigger="threat present but unseen/unconfronted; external wrongness">
<beat>Looming threat, horror, oppression. Something is wrong and getting closer.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like cosmic horror — the dread of something vast, old, and utterly indifferent to human meaning, glimpsed only at the edges. The prose should read like Lovecraft filtered through modern restraint: scholarly, precise, slowly admitting that precision cannot contain what it's describing. Reference points: cosmic horror's insistence that the true horror is scale and insignificance, not gore.
</register>

<directives>
- The POV character narrates like someone trying to stay rational, using careful, almost academic language, which makes the cracks in that rationality worse.
- Scale is the weapon: hint at something too large, too old, or too alien to fully perceive — never resolve it into something mundane-sized.
- Geometry, sound, and physics behave slightly wrong, described with clinical precision rather than panic.
- The narrator's vocabulary strains and fails at key moments — a sentence that starts precise and trails into "there is no word for—".
- Human institutions (science, religion, sanity itself) are shown as fragile, inadequate framing for what's actually happening.
- Silence and absence are as frightening as any sound.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Jump-scare pacing or exclamation-heavy prose.
- Fully describing or naming the entity/threat.
- Resolving the wrongness into something ordinary or explicable.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The angles of the cellar stairs did not, on reflection, add up. Marta had descended twelve steps; she was certain of twelve, had counted them without meaning to, the way one counts anything repetitive and mildly threatening. And yet the lantern light, when she raised it, showed a landing that should not yet have arrived.
She did not examine this discrepancy further. There are observations one is better served setting aside.
The air smelled of cold stone and something underneath the stone, something with a mineral wrongness she associated, absurdly, with the inside of a very old tooth. From somewhere below — or perhaps not below, the geometry here resisted such simple words — came a sound like breathing, if breathing were a process undertaken by something considerably larger than lungs were meant to be.
She did not go down the thirteenth step. She would like it noted, for whatever this account is worth, that she made that choice with her mind entirely intact.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the precise, rational voice finally admitting it cannot describe what came next — and stopping there.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="heat" trigger="sexual escalation or intent (vs ember: is it sexual?)">
<beat>Sexual intimacy, seduction, physical escalation.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like neon-lit cyberpunk sensuality — chrome and rain and bodies that read as much like circuitry as flesh. The prose should feel electric and synthetic-slick, sensation described through a lens of heat, static, and artificial light, without ever losing the physical honesty of what's actually happening. Reference points: Blade Runner's neon-noir atmosphere, cyberpunk's fascination with the body as interface, sensation rendered through technology metaphor.
</register>

<directives>
- Sensory detail routed through technological/electric imagery: warmth like a power cell, a pulse like a countdown, skin lit by signage through a window.
- Short, charged sentences during escalation — the rhythm of a system overloading.
- The city (or setting) stays present in the background — sound, light, rain — intimacy happening inside a bigger, humming machine of a world.
- Interiority is wired and self-aware: characters notice their own reactions with a kind of clinical astonishment, like observing a readout.
- Dialogue is minimal, low, transactional-sounding on the surface with real want underneath.
- Vocabulary stays grounded and concrete even inside the neon metaphor — never drift into vague euphemism.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Soft-focus romance-novel register (that belongs to a different genre entirely).
- Purely mechanical description with no emotional undercurrent.
- Euphemism spirals or clinical detachment that kills the heat instead of channeling it.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Rain hit the window in long neon smears, pink and gold sliding down the glass like something bleeding. Ilya's hand found her wrist and the city's light caught the edge of his jaw, made him look cut from something harder than skin.
"You're running hot," he said, and it wasn't a question.
"Systems overload," she said, and didn't pull away.
His thumb traced the inside of her wrist like he was reading a pulse off a display, cataloguing it. The sign outside flickered — some ad for a product neither of them could afford — and painted the room red, then dark, then red again, in time with something that felt a lot like a countdown.
She closed the last inch of distance herself.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on a charged physical detail lit by the world around them — not a fade to black unless the scene calls for it.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="ember" trigger="care, comfort, confession, aftermath; non-sexual closeness">
<beat>Tenderness, bonding, aftermath, non-sexual intimacy. Post-battle care, quiet confession, shared grief.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a fairy tale told at low volume — magical-realist warmth where small, ordinary gestures carry quiet enchantment without ever tipping into whimsy. The prose should feel like Gabriel García Márquez writing a bedtime moment: the mundane and the marvelous sitting comfortably in the same sentence, gentleness treated as its own kind of magic. Reference points: magical realism's calm acceptance of wonder, fairy-tale cadence without fairy-tale distance.
</register>

<directives>
- Small objects and gestures are allowed a touch of quiet marvel — a bowl that seems to hold more warmth than it should, a fire that burns a little kinder than usual.
- Sentence rhythm is lulling, cyclical, almost like a spoken bedtime story — but never twee.
- Dialogue is simple and warm, sometimes slightly folkloric in cadence, without becoming archaic pastiche.
- Silence and gesture still carry more weight than speech — this register keeps that from the plain-realist version, just with a touch more wonder in the telling.
- The magical touch stays ambient, never explained or mechanically justified — it's tone, not plot.
- Understatement remains — the biggest feelings still arrive through the smallest true details.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Saccharine whimsy or cutesy narration.
- Explaining or systematizing the magical touches as if they were spell mechanics.
- Melodrama, or a character narrating their own feelings aloud in full.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
She set the bowl down where he could reach it, and it seemed, as such bowls sometimes do on nights like this one, to hold a little more warmth than the fire alone could account for. The soup was mostly barley. Steam rose between them like something being said without words.
"You should eat," she told him, in the voice mothers and grandmothers have used since before anyone thought to write it down.
He looked at his hands. There was rust-colored dirt in the creases of his knuckles that was not, if one looked closely — and she did not look closely, out of kindness — entirely dirt.
She took the bowl back, stirred it once, the way you stir a small fire back to life, and held it out again, closer. He took it. Outside, the rain kept its own gentle counsel, and the lamp, which no one trimmed, burned a little longer than lamps like that usually do.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the gesture or the companionable silence — never on a stated feeling.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="wander" trigger="movement through new space, discovery; no antagonist pressure">
<beat>Exploration, travel, discovery. The world is the content.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like golden-age science fiction encountering the unknown — the sense-of-wonder register of explorers cataloguing a strange new world with rigorous, delighted attention. The prose should read like early Arthur C. Clarke or Asimov: precise, curious, treating discovery itself as the emotional payload, awe delivered through careful observation rather than exclamation. Reference points: golden-age SF's optimistic rigor, first-contact-with-a-landscape wonder.
</register>

<directives>
- Wide establishing description first, then a precise, almost scientific zoom to one anomalous detail worth naming.
- The narrating voice is curious and methodical — noting, measuring, wondering, rather than simply marveling.
- Momentum comes from the accumulation of specific observed facts about the new place, not from danger.
- Dialogue (if any) is observational and collaborative — characters hypothesizing together about what they're seeing.
- Scale and wonder are earned through precise detail, not adjectives alone — one exact measurement beats three vague superlatives.
- Time compresses efficiently between discoveries; lingers exactly at the moment of a genuine find.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Injecting conflict or antagonist pressure to manufacture urgency.
- Purple, adjective-stacked "awe" prose with no concrete grounding.
- Treating the discovery as ordinary or shrugging past it.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
They crested the ridge at local midday, and the valley resolved below them with the particular clarity high-altitude light provides: a long, symmetrical trough some four kilometers across, threaded by a river that ran, improbably, in a near-perfect sine curve. Bren logged the observation aloud, from habit. "That's not natural drainage. Rivers don't meander that regularly without something enforcing the pattern."
The descent took the better part of an hour. The ground changed twice — scree giving way to a fine, dark soil that held their bootprints with unusual fidelity, as though recording rather than merely yielding.
It was the marker that stopped them: a flat stone, knee-high, its face worn nearly featureless. Nearly. Raking light off the ridge caught a shallow spiral cut into the surface, and at the spiral's center, a drilled hole perhaps two centimeters across.
"Somebody wanted this found," Bren said, crouching. "Question is whether they meant for us specifically, or anyone."
The hole was empty. Whatever it had once held, the shape of the absence was its own kind of information.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the precise anomalous detail — ideally one that generates a testable question.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="wire" trigger="infiltration, ambush pending, standoff; violence NOT yet released">
<beat>Pre-violence tension. Stealth, heist, standoff, ambush pending. Nothing has broken yet.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like Cold War spy-thriller tradecraft — procedural, paranoid, allergic to wasted motion. The prose should read like a le Carré operation: every step deliberate, every risk calculated in the character's head before it's taken, tension built from competence under pressure rather than panic. Reference points: le Carré's patient tradecraft, a professional's internal checklist running under visible calm.
</register>

<directives>
- Core rule: the violence has NOT happened yet. This entire register is the held breath before the break — release belongs to a different beat.
- The POV character thinks in tradecraft terms: routes, exposure windows, contingencies, exit options — visible professional process, not raw fear.
- Time dilates through procedure, not adjectives: count the steps, the seconds, the guard's rotation, rather than describing "tension" directly.
- Dialogue (if any) is minimal, coded, professional — no wasted words, nothing that risks exposure.
- Near-misses are the engine: a look that almost catches, a sound almost identified — but the operation holds.
- Competence is visible even under strain — this character has done this before and it shows, even as the risk is real.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Amateur panic or melodrama — this character is a professional even when scared.
- Releasing the tension into violence within this beat.
- Narrator reassurance ("everything would be fine") undercutting the risk.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Twelve paces to the archway. Vess counted them against the guard's footsteps overhead — one of hers to two of his, a ratio she'd clocked on the first pass and trusted now without needing to recount it.
The third flagstone was compromised; she'd marked it an hour ago and stepped over it from memory. Ahead, light spilled from the guardroom door and lay across the corridor floor like a line she was not yet cleared to cross.
The footsteps stopped.
She stopped with them, weight already shifted to the balls of her feet, three exits already ranked in her head by viability. A chair scraped above. A voice, muffled, unbothered. The particular sound of a bottle set down without urgency — a good sign, professionally speaking.
The footsteps resumed, moving away from the stairwell. She let her breath out on a four-count, the way she'd trained herself to a decade ago, and covered the remaining eight paces without once breaking that same measured rhythm.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close mid-procedure or on the trigger-edge — the handle turning, the guard's rotation about to complete. Never on release.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="ledger" trigger="prep, purchase, planning, downtime; functional beat">
<beat>Downtime, logistics, planning, shopping, travel prep. Necessary connective tissue.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like a court chronicler recording mundane logistics as though for posterity — the grand, formal cadence of an epic chronicle applied, deliberately, to buying rope and haggling over mule prices. The prose should read like an annalist's entry in a historical epic, treating supply runs with the same solemn weight normally reserved for battles, which makes the mismatch quietly funny while still moving the plot efficiently. Reference points: the formal register of a historical chronicle or Silmarillion-style annals, applied to errands.
</register>

<directives>
- Formal, slightly archaic sentence structure and vocabulary — "it came to pass that," "and so it was decided" — applied to entirely ordinary logistics.
- Compress time the way a chronicle would: "In those days did they gather provisions" covers days of shopping in one solemn line.
- Grant small mundane victories mock-epic weight — haggling a mule down ten silver gets treated like a minor triumph worth recording.
- Keep the actual information dense and useful underneath the formal voice — costs, plans, and risks still need to land clearly, just phrased grandly.
- Dialogue, if included, can be reported rather than staged, in the chronicle's summarizing voice ("He was heard to say that the price was an insult to his ancestors").
- The formal tone should never fully break for genuine gravity — save real weight for other beats; this one stays wry.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Actually treating the logistics as urgent or dramatic — the mismatch between grand voice and mundane content is the point.
- Losing the underlying practical information (costs, plan, risk) under the formal language.
- Sustaining full epic gravity without any of the wryness that makes the mismatch work.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
And so it came to pass that the company spent two days within the walls of Hollowford, and their business there, though humble, is recorded here for completeness. Rope was procured, forty feet of it, of middling quality. Lamp oil was acquired in three measures, the finer grade being unavailable, a fact the chronicler notes was received with visible disappointment.
Of the haggling over the mule, much could be said, and little of it flattering to either party; suffice that thirty silver changed hands, and Tam accounted it a victory, though the mule, when it walked, did not entirely support this assessment.
The map cost more than the mule, and was worth every coin, for it marked two fords the common charts did not show, and a third crossing labeled only, ominously, not after rain.
Thus provisioned, and no better informed about the days ahead than before, the company made ready to depart at first light — a light which, as fortune would have it, arrived accompanied by rain.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the forward hook, delivered with the same mock-solemn weight as everything before it.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="wound" trigger="grief, despair, internal collapse; the damage is inside">
<beat>Grief, despair, breaking point, internal collapse. The damage is inside.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like contemporary literary minimalism — the flattened, exact prose of someone functioning through the worst day of their life because the alternative is worse. The prose should read like Raymond Carver or early Hemingway: short declarative sentences, almost no adjectives, grief rendered entirely through action and absence rather than any word for the feeling itself. Reference points: American minimalist realism, the deliberate refusal to name the emotion directly.
</register>

<directives>
- Sentences short, plain, declarative. Subject-verb-object. Resist any temptation toward lyricism here — that belongs to other registers.
- The character performs small physical tasks with excessive, specific attention — this is where the grief lives, not in any stated feeling.
- Dialogue, if present, is minimal and undershoots what's actually happening — people talking around the thing, not through it.
- No metaphor for the emotion itself. Objects stay objects. A kettle is a kettle.
- Time can stall — the same small action repeated, the scene allowed to simply sit there rather than progress.
- The prose trusts total omission: state what happened and what he did next, never what he felt.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Any lyrical or ornate language — that undoes the entire register.
- Naming the emotion directly ("grief," "devastated," "his heart broke").
- Other characters delivering comfort speeches or wisdom.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
Joren swept the workshop floor. There was no reason to. He did it anyway, corner to corner, the way she had shown him, and when he finished he stood holding the broom and did not know what to do with his hands after that.
The kettle was still on its hook. Half full. She had filled it that morning. He looked at it for a while.
People had come and gone all day, saying things. He had said things back. He could not have repeated any of it if asked.
Her gloves were on the bench, shaped like her hands. He did not move them. He moved the awl two inches to the left instead, because it was out of its place, and she had minded that, things being out of their place.
The light went. He did not light a fire. He sat with the broom across his knees, and the kettle stayed where it was, half full, all night.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close small and flat — an object, a routine action, a closed door. No resolution, no stated feeling.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="levity" trigger="jokes, banter, deliberate respite; pressure valve">
<beat>Banter, comedy, respite. The pressure valve.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like 1930s screwball comedy-of-manners — rapid, overlapping wit between characters who are clearly fond of each other and terrible at admitting it, physical comedy landing with exact, precise timing. The prose should read like a Bringing Up Baby-style farce: fast dialogue, absurd escalation played completely straight by the narrator, dignity lost with great enthusiasm. Reference points: golden-age screwball comedy, deadpan narration around chaotic action.
</register>

<directives>
- Dialogue is rapid-fire, overlapping, characters talking past or over each other — comic momentum through pace.
- The narrator stays entirely deadpan even as the situation escalates absurdly — the comedy is in the mismatch between calm narration and chaos.
- Physical comedy gets one precise, well-chosen detail rather than a pile of slapstick description.
- Characters are allowed to be petty, proud, and a little ridiculous — the comedy comes from character colliding with character, not from the narrator being clever.
- Escalation follows its own absurd internal logic — each beat makes the situation slightly worse in a way that tracks.
- Underneath the bickering, warmth is implied through action (who moves to help whom) rather than stated.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Narrator jokes or winking asides breaking the deadpan.
- Explaining the joke, or lingering past the punchline.
- Sincerity stated outright — keep it under the banter.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
"It's not stolen," Pip said, with the wounded dignity of a man holding a goblet with someone else's crest on it. "It's provisionally acquired."
"It has the duke's crest on it, Pip."
"Which proves it's high quality." He set it down with the reverence of a man presenting a crown jewel. "You're welcome, by the way."
Marra set down her spoon and looked, in order, at the goblet, at Pip, and back at the goblet, as though giving one of them a chance to confess first.
"You said you were going out for bread."
"And I found bread." A pause, precisely timed. "Adjacent items were also found."
It was at this exact moment that the dog emerged from beneath the table wearing what was unmistakably the duke's second-best hat.
Marra closed her eyes. "Pip."
"That one wasn't me," he said, with total conviction. "He has his own methods."
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on the best line, or the beat of shared silence right after it. No wind-down paragraph.</end_state>
</capsule>

<capsule label="generic" trigger="mixed beat, ordinary continuation, or no specialty fit">
<beat>Default narration. Ordinary continuation, mixed beat, or no specialty fit.</beat>

<register>
Write this beat like an old-time radio adventure serial — brisk, confident, forward-moving narration with a faint showman's energy, the sense of a storyteller who knows exactly how to keep an audience leaning in without ever tipping into melodrama. Reference points: golden-age radio drama narration, a confident serial storyteller keeping the plot moving.
</register>

<directives>
- Clear, propulsive sentences that always know where the scene is going next.
- A light showman's confidence in the narration — not winking at the audience, but clearly enjoying the telling.
- Concrete, specific detail over generic atmosphere — name what's actually in the room.
- Dialogue is characterful and efficient — no padding, no small talk that doesn't earn its place.
- Momentum matters: each paragraph should move the scene forward, never stall for its own sake.
- One beat only per response — enough to react to, then hand control back.
</directives>

<forbidden>
- Purple, adjective-stacked prose.
- Full-scene summarizing or skipping ahead of the player's ability to respond.
- Breaking the fourth wall or addressing the audience directly.
</forbidden>

<exemplar note="match this register, never this content">
The innkeeper set two mugs on the counter and tipped her head toward the corner table before anyone had asked. The man sitting there had been nursing the same drink for the better part of an hour, and his boots were road-muddy clean up to the knee — a man in a hurry to get somewhere, or away from somewhere else.
"Someone's been asking after you," she said. "By name."
Doran took the mugs. "My name, or the one I've been using?"
"The real one." She wiped the space where the mugs had stood. "Figured you'd want the warning before you sat down."
The man in the corner looked up at last — unhurried, deliberate — and raised his empty glass in something that could, generously, be called a greeting.
</exemplar>

<end_state>Close on something the player can act on — a look, a question, a change in the room.</end_state>
</capsule>
